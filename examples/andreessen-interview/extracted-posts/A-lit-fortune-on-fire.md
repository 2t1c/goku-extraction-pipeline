# Example post — A. Lit fortune on fire

A worked Tier 1 example. The hero-arc shape: friend warns → ignored → catastrophe → vindication.

## Post body (final shipped version)

```
Marc Andreessen says Elon almost lit his entire $180 million fortune on fire making rockets.

His friend tried to stop him.

The friend (Andreessen thinks it was Adeo Ressi) sat Elon down before he started SpaceX.

He showed him a compilation, pre-YouTube, of rockets blowing up over and over again.

"You're literally going to light your fortune on fire."

This was 2002. Elon had just sold PayPal.

The $180 million he walked out with was everything he had left after taxes.

He spent it on SpaceX anyway.

The first three rockets exploded.

The famous photo from this era shows Elon in shorts and a polo.

He's crouched in front of the wreckage of the third rocket.

Eric Berger documented the entire period in his book *Liftoff*.

Andreessen's summary:

"Nothing good in the book. It's just reading one failure after another, and one catastrophe after another."

Eventually the rockets started landing on their butt.

Today, SpaceX builds its own city in Texas.

Starlink came later — a side project at the rocket company.

The friend who tried to save Elon's fortune was wrong about the rockets.

But he was right about the fire.

What "you're going to light your fortune on fire" warning have you ignored — and were glad you did?

If you're new here, follow @GeniusGTX for content on the greatest minds in economics, psychology, and history.

P.S. I made a full playbook breaking down the timeless decision-making mental models used by history's greatest thinkers.

So if you want to stop overthinking, control chaos, and navigate any decision with the clarity...

Comment "models" and follow @GeniusGTX so I can DM you a copy.

— Marc Andreessen ( @pmarca ), co-founder of a16z, on David Senra's ( @FoundersPodcast ) podcast
```

## Why this works

- **Hook (15 words at limit):** named subject (Elon) + specific number ($180M) + dramatic reversal ("almost lit on fire"). Verb is `says`.
- **Hedge preserved:** "Andreessen thinks it was Adeo Ressi" — Andreessen hedges ("I think it was Adeo Ressi, or I forgot the friend") and the post mirrors that hedge. **Don't strip hedges from the source.**
- **Specific number anchor:** $180M, three rockets, 2002. Specifics make the claim feel researched.
- **Verbatim quote pacing:** opens with the friend's warning quote, mid-post drops the *Liftoff* summary quote. Both verbatim.
- **The closing reversal:** "He was wrong about the rockets. But he was right about the fire." — paradox, lands with finality, recasts the title in two beats.
- **Engagement Q:** opinion + personal — note this Q is longer than the new "simple binary" rule prefers. **Current rule (May 2026) is to use simpler binary** (e.g. *"Brave or reckless?"*). This example shows the older question style; future drafts use binary.

## Clip spec

- **Range:** 1:45:01–1:46:50 (~1:49)
- **Start cue (verbatim):** "There's that famous photo."
- **End cue (verbatim):** "...method that he has been working on and refining for coming on 30 years that has worked better than anybody's method."
- **Slug:** `andreessen-elon-lit-fortune-on-fire`

## What the agent should learn from this

1. **Preserve hedges from the source.** If the speaker says "I think it was Adeo Ressi," the post says "Andreessen thinks it was Adeo Ressi" — not "Adeo Ressi sat Elon down."
2. **Anchor with a specific number** in the hook ($180M) when the source provides one.
3. **End on a paradox** if the source supports it — "wrong about the rockets, right about the fire."
4. **Engagement Q rule has updated.** This example uses the older long-form question. Use simple binary today.
